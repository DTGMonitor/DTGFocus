SensiMap quick guide

  1. Add terrain data — drop a .dtm, .dxf, .str, .xyz, .csv or ESRI .asc onto the window, or use Data Sources ▸ Add terrain data. Point files get gridded; DXF 3DFACE / polyface meshes and Surpac DTM+STR triangulations are rasterised. No file? Press Load demo open pit.
  2. Place a radar — pick Radar positions ▸ Radar 1 in the Layers tree, type coordinates in Properties, or arm the Place radar tool and click the model.
  3. Choose the movement vector — Processing tab: steepest / horizontal / vertical / normal, or a custom trend & plunge for a known failure mechanism. A custom vector can also be given as an offset flatter than the steepest line, which keeps each cell's own dip direction.
  4. Or let the structure choose it — Structure tab: type the mapped dips and dip directions (or pick three points off the surface), set the slope face and friction angle, and the wedge analysis intersects every pair of planes. Press → on a critical wedge and trace the block it would release: inside that polygon the model uses the wedge's own line of intersection instead of the global assumption.
  5. Compute — sensitivity = |cos θ| between the radar line of sight and the assumed movement vector; 1 = fully measurable, 0 = invisible to the radar.
  6. Plan — add several radar positions and read the ranking table in Statistics to find the best site (lowest in the pit usually wins, as long as it is not in the way).

Remember: the sensitivity map is a starting point — it does not know the real failure mechanism.

Data sources & gridding

Cell sizeRaster resolution in metres. Blank = derived from Target cells. Aim for ≈ ⅓ of your berm width — coarser than that and benches average into a smooth ramp, which biases steepest sensitivity.
Target cellsCells along the longer side when Cell size is blank. 320 fast · 1000 detailed · ceiling ≈ 2.2 M nodes.
Search radiusHow far to hunt for source points when filling a cell. Raise for sparse data.
InterpCombining several points in one cell. IDW weights by 1/d² (default), Nearest preserves original values, Mean is smoothest.
Fill small holes
Fill passes		Fills empty cells from ≥ 4 valid neighbours; each pass grows the fill by one cell. Never modifies cells that already have data.
Smooth normalsOff by default — it averages elevations and erases real bench edges. Only for noisy photogrammetry/laser data.

Radar positions

Easting / Northing / LevelSensor coordinates in your mine grid. The Place radar tool sets X/Y by clicking.
Ant. heightMetres added to the terrain when Z = terrain + antenna height is ticked — mast or vehicle height.
Boresight azWhere the antenna faces: degrees from grid north, clockwise (0 = N, 90 = E). Ignored while Auto-aim is on.
Boresight elAntenna tilt. The elevation the boresight points at; positive = aimed up. A sensor on a pit floor watching a high wall is tilted up, and the elevation cone follows it. Leaving this at 0 with a narrow El scan width is the usual reason a whole wall reads out of scan. Auto-aim sets it at the model centre.
Az scan width
El scan width		Total angular coverage, exactly as printed on the datasheet. 90 means ±45° either side of the boresight. Az 360 = no azimuth limit. El width is measured around the tilt above, not around the horizon.
Range min / maxInstrument range gates in metres. Range min is what creates the blind wedge directly under the antenna.
Multi-sensor combinationSelected = one sensor · Best of all = max sensitivity per cell · Average · Which is best = colour by winning sensor.

Sensitivity model

Movement vectorWhatever is chosen here is the assumption outside any structural domain — a domain drawn in the Structure tab overrides it inside its own polygon. Steepest/Horizontal/Normal are recomputed per cell from the local surface; Vertical/Custom apply one global direction to the whole model. Normal assumes each facet moves straight out of itself (bulging, buckling, rockfall), so its sensitivity map is identical to the amplitude proxy.
Trend az / PlungeCustom vector only: azimuth from north clockwise, plunge below horizontal. Plunge 90 = straight down.
Flatter byCustom vector only, and the alternative to typing a trend: the vector keeps each cell's dip direction and plunges this many degrees less than the local steepest line — the common case of a failure daylighting 10–15° flatter than the face. 0° is identical to Steepest, and an offset that reaches the local slope angle is identical to Horizontal; nothing tips above the horizontal. Being per cell, it is the one custom vector that is not a single global direction.
Occlusion testMarches the sight line over the terrain and flags hidden cells as shadowed. A finer grid finds more shadow — resolved bench crests really do block the beam.
Accuracy / ToleranceSampling step along the ray (in cells), and how far terrain must rise above the ray to count as blocking. Raise the tolerance if noise causes speckled shadows.
Grazing rejectionDrops cells the beam only skims. 90° incidence = line of sight parallel to the surface: fine geometry, no usable return.
ThresholdSensitivity you consider alarm-worthy. Drives usable area, the position ranking and the colour-bar marker.
Displ. factorA hypothetical true movement in mm. The probe and the Measurable displacement layer show sensitivity × this value — what the radar would actually register.

Stretch — reshaping a boundary you already drew

The first trace is never quite right. One tool reshapes all three kinds of boundary — selection regions, structural domains and the measurement — because from your side they are the same gesture on the same kind of thing.

Moving the whole thingEvery ring also carries a centre handle at its centroid. Grab that and the whole boundary follows the pointer, keeping its shape exactly — which is what makes a duplicated polygon useful. Escape puts every vertex back, not just one. It is not a vertex, so Delete does not apply to it.
What has handlesSelection regions, structural domains and the measurement have one handle per vertex, plus the centre handle above. A mapped plane is not a polygon — it is an anchor, an orientation and a size — so it gets five handles instead: the four corners resize it and the centre moves it. Resizing scales by how far the pointer travels out from the centre, exactly like a scale handle anywhere else, so it needs no ground under it and feels the same however the plane is oriented. The centre is the one handle that has to land on the surface — drop it over the sky and the plane stays where it was. A plane that was typed rather than picked has an orientation but no location, so nothing is drawn for it and there is nothing to grab; use ⌖ on its row in the Structure tab to place it on the model first.
AimingHandles are picked on screen, not on the ground: a plane's corner stands clear of the terrain, so the ray under your cursor lands somewhere else entirely and a distance measured in survey coordinates would look for it in the wrong place. Point at the handle you can see. A corner silhouetted against the sky, with no terrain behind it at all, still picks up.
The gestureClick–move–click, not drag: dragging already means orbit in the 3D view, and a tool that fights the camera for the left button fights you. Click a vertex to pick it up, move the pointer and watch the boundary follow, click again to drop it there.
Adding and removingClick the middle of an edge to insert a vertex there and pick it straight up. Delete or Backspace while holding one takes it out. A boundary is never whittled below three points — remove the whole thing instead.
Putting it backEsc returns a held vertex to where it came from and leaves you in the tool; a second Esc leaves the tool. Switching tools with a vertex in hand drops it where it is rather than leaving it stuck to the pointer.
What updates whenThe outline follows the pointer live; the shading, the mask, the domain's cell index and the statistics are all rebuilt on the drop. Both are passes over the raster, so doing them at pointer rate would make the stretch crawl. That is why the shading briefly disappears while you are holding a vertex — showing the old shape under the new outline would read as a bug.
RenamingA region is renamed in Properties when it is the selected tree node; a domain, in the name field on its card in the Structure tab. Both names carry into the layer tree, the status messages and the saved project. Clear one and it falls back to its position in the list.

Measure — checking the model against itself

The ruler answers the question every slope engineer asks of a new surface: are these numbers right? It measures nothing the model does not already know, which is exactly why it is a check.

Taking oneArm Measure and click along the ground. Enter finishes the line; clicking back on the first point closes it and unlocks the areas; Backspace removes the last point; Esc abandons it. A finished measurement stays on screen until you clear it with the ×, so you can orbit around and keep reading it.
Per legBearing from grid north, plan distance, height difference (signed), slope distance and inclination — negative downhill, so the sign tells you which way the leg was taken.
Plan / slope /
ground length		Plan ignores height. Slope is the straight line through the air. Ground is walked over the raster, so on a benched wall it is much longer than the chord — the × factor beside it is a direct read on how broken the ground is. In the 3D view the solid line is the ground walk and the faint straight one is the chord.
Plan / surface areaClose the measurement and it reports the plan area of the ring, and the true surface area of the cells inside it — each cell's plan area divided by the cosine of its slope. A steep face holds far more rock than its outline suggests, and the × factor is how much more.
The slope checkTwo independent routes to the same figure. From areas is the dip a single uniform plane would need to have this much surface over this much plan. Raster mean is the arithmetic mean of the slope layer's own values over the same cells. When they agree, the slope layer is behaving over that patch; when they disagree by more than 5° the tool says so, because the ground in there is not one wall and no single slope figure describes it — measure a smaller patch.
HolesA ground walk that crosses a gap in the survey carries the last known level across it and reports how many samples it bridged. That length is a floor, not a measurement.

Structure — mapped discontinuities and kinematic analysis

A sensitivity map is only as good as the movement direction fed into it. This tab derives that direction from the structure instead of assuming it.

Slope face
dip / dip dir		The wall being assessed. Everything below asks whether a block can escape from this face, so it has to be right. From probed cell takes it off the survey at the point you last identified; From selection mask averages the surface orientation over the mask — vector mean of the normals, and it warns you when the mask spans more than one wall and no single face orientation exists.
Friction φFriction angle of the joint surfaces, degrees. Both planes of a wedge are assumed to share it, as in Dips. It is the only strength parameter in a kinematic test: nothing here is a factor of safety.
Placing and sizing⌖ on a row places that plane on the model — required before a typed orientation is drawn at all, and the way to move a picked one somewhere else. Once placed, the Stretch vertices tool moves it by its centre and resizes it by any corner; a plane sized that way keeps its own size and ↺ hands it back to the panel default. None of it touches the analysis, which reads dip and dip direction only.
Rectangle or traceTwo different things, and Draw as chooses between them. The rectangle is a symbol standing in the air where the structure lies — useful for reading its orientation, but it hangs in front of the face and clutters the view. The trace is the line where the plane actually cuts the ground: what a geologist would walk and flag, and the only part of a structure a surface really shows. It is computed as the contour where the plane meets the survey, clipped to the rectangle's own extent so an unbounded plane cannot claim ground nobody mapped. Trace on the surface only gives you the line and nothing else. Hide everything in front of the face keeps the rectangle but cuts it back to the part inside the rock — fill and outline both, so nothing hangs in the air — and turning the surface down or off in the View tab is what lets you see what is left.
How planes are drawnA plane picked off the surface is drawn where it was picked, as a rectangle lying in the structure: long edges along the strike, short edges down the dip, and a tick from the centre pointing down-dip so you can tell which way it falls. Plane size sets the strike length in metres (blank scales it to the model), Plane fill % its opacity — 0 leaves it as an outline — and each row's swatch its colour. All three are symbols only: nothing in the analysis reads them.
All in, all outThe tick box in the header of the plane list puts every discontinuity into the analysis or leaves them all out — without deleting any of them, so the set is still there to switch back on. It shows a middle state when only some are in. The domain list has the same switch above its cards, reading n of m active, and both groups in the Layers tree carry the same tick box with a n / m count beside it. All three drive the same thing, so they cannot disagree.
Renaming and copyingA plane's name cell is the rename field — click it and type. Copy on its row duplicates it, same orientation, offset so it can be seen and moved; one joint set outcrops in several places and each wants its own patch. The same two gestures apply everywhere: a domain is renamed in the name field on its card and copied with Copy in that card's action row; a region is renamed and duplicated in Properties once it is the selected tree node.
DiscontinuitiesDip and dip direction per structure. Type them, Paste list… a column straight out of a mapping spreadsheet, or arm Pick on model and click three or more points where the structure outcrops — a plane is least-squares fitted through them and the scatter it reports is what tells you whether the trace really was planar. Untick a plane to leave it out of the analysis without deleting it.
StereonetLower hemisphere, equal-angle (Wulff) by default because that is what Dips uses for kinematic analysis; equal-area (Schmidt) is there for pole density work. A plane is a great circle, a pole is one dot, and a wedge is the line of intersection of two planes. Hover for a read-out of the orientation under the cursor, or of the symbol you are pointing at.
Wedge sliding
critical zones		Red — sliding on both planes. Inside the friction cone and outside the slope plane: the wedge axis daylights out of the face and plunges steeper than φ. Amber — sliding on one plane. Between the slope plane and a great circle inclined at φ: the wedge axis itself is flatter than φ, but one of the two joints dips steeper than φ and can release on its own, so the movement direction reported is that joint's dip vector rather than the wedge axis. No lateral limits are applied — the second plane frees the wedge to break out sideways.
Friction coneA circle at plunge φ. The friction angle is measured in from the rim, so anything plotting inside the circle plunges more steeply than φ.
Is the wedge really there?A stereonet knows orientations and nothing else. It will rank two structures mapped four hundred metres apart as a critical wedge, and be perfectly correct about a wedge that exists nowhere in the pit. Once planes have locations, the Meets column answers what the stereonet cannot, and it distinguishes four different ways of being no risk:
184 mthe real thing — that much rock behind the face, and the line reaches daylight, so it can release
buriedit bounds rock, but the line does not reach the face inside the extents these planes are drawn with. Stretch the patches down-plunge and look again
in airthe two do cross — out in front of the face, above the ground. There is no rock between them, so there is nothing to fail. Only the survey can tell you this
apartas mapped, the two never touch at all
—a plane has no location, so none of it can be told. Place it with ⌖
The table is sorted by that column first and by the kinematic zone only within it, so everything that could fail sits above everything that cannot, with a divider where the two bands meet. — ranks above in air deliberately: not knowing is not the same as knowing there is nothing, and burying an untested pair under a ruled-out one would hide the very thing that needs a location adding. Only wedges with rock in them drops apart and in air. A buried pair is kept — there is rock there — and so is an untestable one. The segment is drawn in the 3D view under Annotations ▸ Plane intersections: the part inside the rock in the zone colour (red on both planes, amber on one, grey stable) and the part standing in the air faint, which says why a critical pair was set aside faster than any table can.
Results tableEvery plane pair, critical or not — the near misses matter, because a few degrees of change in the face orientation moves them. Hover a row to pick it out on the stereonet; press → to load its movement direction into the domain builder.
ExportResults CSV is the sheet for the geotechnical review: every pair, its intersection, its zone and its movement direction, with the face and φ it was assessed against in the header. Stereonet PNG is the figure.

Structural domains

What it isA drawn polygon that carries its own movement vector. Cells inside it are modelled as moving that way; everything outside keeps the vector chosen in Processing. That is what lets a mapped wedge and the wall around it be assessed in one run, each against the direction it would really move — and what turns "this wedge is critical" into "the radar would see 6.4 of every 10 mm this wedge moves".
Where its edge sitsThe boundary is the zero of one field: how far inside the block a point is, taken as the closest of the three bounds — above plane A, above plane B, under the height cap. Where each bound is the binding one, that zero is that bound's own line, so along the flanks the block edge and the plane's trace are the same line computed the same way and cannot disagree. Ticking Stop it at the area of interest adds a fourth bound: a region you have drawn becomes the block's outer boundary too.
Area and volumeEach domain card reports its plan area, the rock face over it (each cell's plan area over the cosine of its slope, so a steep block holds far more face than its outline suggests) and, for a block built from two planes, its volume — measured from the surface down to whichever plane is higher at each cell, since the wedge is the rock above both. Mean and maximum thickness come with it, and they matter: a wedge bounded by two planes and a slope face has no upper release surface, so the rock above both planes keeps deepening away from the point it daylights at. The volume is therefore set partly by where you capped the block, not by the structure alone, and the card says so whenever that starts to dominate.
Built, not tracedWith Build the block automatically ticked — the default for a wedge taken from the results table — you click once, at the point where the wedge daylights, and the footprint is worked out. Three things bound it, which is what a wedge is: the two joint planes, both passed through the point you clicked (the block is the rock standing above both of them, which puts their traces on the ground as its flanks); the survey surface, so it follows every bench and berm the wall actually has; and the block height, because on a face steep enough for the wedge to daylight the terrain rises faster than the line of intersection does, so the region never closes off upslope on its own. Only the run connected to the point you clicked is taken — the same test is satisfied on other walls, and those are different wedges. Untick it to trace by hand instead; a trend and plunge typed by hand cannot be built from, because it does not say which planes bound the block.
Making oneTwo steps, and the first is easy to skip. 1. Press Use on a wedge in the results table — its movement direction drops into the builder below, which flashes so you can see it happen — or type a trend and plunge yourself. Until a direction is chosen the draw button stays disabled and the chip beside it says so. 2. Press Draw the block on the model and trace its footprint exactly like an AOI region: Enter, a double-click or a click back on the first vertex closes it.
Then computeThe domain is drawn on the terrain the moment it closes, but the sensitivity does not change until you run the model again — the badge turns to recompute needed and the status line says so. Until then the map on screen still describes the old assumption everywhere.
Reading oneEach card reports, after a compute, how many visible cells the block has, its ground area, the mean and p10 sensitivity over it, and how much of a given true displacement survives projection onto the line of sight. Those figures describe the last compute, so a domain edited since shows none rather than showing numbers that belong to a different shape.
Seeing oneThe footprint is draped on the terrain and, with Annotations ▸ Shade drawn areas on, filled in the domain's colour — set by the swatch on its card, at the opacity given by Domain fill %. The shaded cells are precisely the ones the override applies to. An arrow from the centroid shows the movement direction. Mapped planes picked off the surface are drawn as filled rectangles at the same time, so a wedge reads as two planes meeting in the wall.
Copying oneCopy on a domain card duplicates it — same movement direction, same shape — offset so it can be seen and grabbed. The same wedge often releases in two or three places along a wall, and re-deriving the direction each time is work for nothing. Arm Stretch / move and drag the copy's centre handle to put it where it belongs. Regions duplicate the same way, from Duplicate in their Properties strip.
OverlapsLater domains win, so a small detailed block drawn over a broad one behaves the way the drawing order reads.
Not the AOIDomains and the area of interest are different things. The AOI decides which cells the statistics describe; a domain decides which direction a cell is assumed to move. They can be used together and neither affects the other.

Area of interest

X/Y/Z bounds, slope bandRestrict statistics and position ranking to the slope you care about. Outside cells are dimmed, not deleted. The regions replace the X/Y rectangle (which then follows their combined bounding box); Z and slope keep applying on top. Clear regions, or editing an X/Y box by hand, returns to the rectangle. Note it masks the analysis, not the gridding.
Draw regionTraces a boundary by clicking vertex after vertex on the model — Enter, a double-click or a click back on the first vertex closes it, Backspace undoes a vertex, Esc cancels. Arm it again to add another region: two walls, a wall and a dump, whatever the analysis is about.
ShadingWith Annotations ▸ Shade drawn areas on, a drawn region is painted onto the terrain as well as outlined. The shading is built from the raster, so what you see shaded is exactly the cells the statistics count — a region that clips a bench edge, or that loses cells to the Z or slope filters, looks clipped rather than smooth. The plain X/Y rectangle is never shaded: everything outside it is already dimmed.
Region listOne row per region under Area of interest in the tree, with its vertex count, masked cells and ground area. Hover a row to pick that region out in the 3D view, or use its × to remove it. Regions are joined, never subtracted — the statistics, histogram and position ranking describe every cell inside any of them, and a cell shared by two overlapping regions is counted once, against the first one drawn.

Visibility1 = the sensor sees it, 0 = shadowed.

Terrain surfaceView tab. Show terrain surface hides the mesh outright — everything drawn on it keeps drawing, so the structures, domains, sensors and a measurement can be read against nothing at all. Untick it, or press Revert to layer colours, to go back. While either is on the colour bar hides itself, because a scale nothing is being coloured by is worse than none.

Statistics & histogram

analysed / visibleCells inside the selection mask, and how many of those the sensor actually sees. Every figure below describes the visible ones only.
mean / medianAverage and middle sensitivity. A mean well above the median means a few excellent facets are carrying an otherwise poor position.
p10 / p90 · p20 / p80Percentiles of the same distribution. p10 is the value a tenth of the wall falls below — the worst ground you still have to live with. p20 / p80 give the tighter middle band, far less swayed by a handful of outlying cells than p10 / p90, which is the pair to quote when comparing two candidate positions.
HistogramThe distribution those figures summarise. The dashed white line is the alarm threshold. Hover a bar for its sensitivity band, cell count, share of the visible cells, ground area and the cumulative share at or below it.
